- source_spec: `spec-4a-report-types.md`
  summary: One stray `$` beside one stray `€` in an otherwise plain number column takes the whole column to `text`, because "a column mixing two affixes is text" is unconditional.
  evidence: The Boundaries are unqualified — "A column mixing two affixes is `text` plus a warning naming both" — and the implementation follows them: two affixes each carried by at least one value that reads as a number disqualify the number reading for the whole column. - source_spec: `spec-4a-report-types.md`
  summary: Two ISO 8601 shapes this candidate refuses that nothing has ever weighed — **ordinal dates** (`2025-001`) and **leap seconds** (`2025-12-31T23:59:60Z`) — plus the shapes that were weighed, listed here so "the one shape nobody has weighed" stops being a claim nobody enumerated.
  evidence: Restated 2026-08-03, three times, and this version replaces a false completeness claim of the reviewer's own: the entry said ordinal dates are "the one ISO 8601 shape this candidate refuses that nobody has ever weighed", and `2025-12-31T23:59:60Z` reads as `text` — measured — while nothing anywhere has ever decided about it. The enumeration, so the next reader checks a list rather than a sentence. **Taken** (all measured `datetime`, `settled`): extended date-and-time on four candidates; basic format `20251231T1430` with seconds, fraction and offset optional; lowercase `t`/`z`; the comma decimal; offsets `Z`, `±HH`, `±HHmm`, `±HH:mm` bounded at ±14:00 with `-00:00` refused as the standard refuses it; end-of-day `24:00` where minutes, seconds and any fraction are all zero. **Cut before this story, and not reopened**: week dates `2025-W01-1` and quarters — period labels rather than instants, on the closed triage entry below; a week needs weekday arithmetic twice over, to validate (2025 has 52 ISO weeks and 2026 has 53, and you cannot see which by looking) and to convert (`2025-W01-1` is 30 December **2024**). **Open, never weighed, both measured `text`**: (1) ordinal dates — `2025-001` is 1 January and `2024-366` is 31 December, so bounding one is the leap-year rule this file already contains and converting one is the year's first day plus n−1, no weekday arithmetic anywhere; (2) leap seconds — `23:59:60` is one *second* value exactly as `24:00` is one *hour* value, it needs no calendar arithmetic to read, and real systems emit it (UTC has had 27 of them, and a system logging in UTC at the moment of one writes it). Both are cheap to *read* and neither is free to *convert*, which is the same reason both wait. **Also refused and deliberately out of scope, listed so they are not rediscovered as findings**: reduced precision (`2025-12`, `2025`) is a period label like a week; ISO durations (`P1Y2M3D`) and intervals (`2025-01-01/2025-01-31`) are not instants at all and this story's `duration` is `HH:mm(:ss)` by the Boundaries.
  status: open and small, both halves. Not an Ask First about the wording — the Boundaries say what is in and why — but a real Source carrying either shape decides it, exactly as one carrying month names opened story 4b. Whoever takes them should take them with story 6, where the conversion lives: an ordinal date is a day count into a year and a leap second is a second the epoch does not have, so both are conversion questions wearing a reading question's clothes. **Do not implement leap seconds from this entry alone** — the reason they are here is that the completeness claim above was false, not that the shape has been decided.

- source_spec: `spec-4-xlsx-parquet-sources.md`

- source_spec: `spec-3-typing-step-zero.md`
  summary: Detection blocks the main thread for **roughly 2.6× what it cost before story 4a** at the NFR-3 per-Source target of 100,000 rows by 20 columns, and again on every encoding or header-row re-read. **The ratio is the finding and the absolute depends on the column mix, so no single second-figure belongs in this line** — the two mixes measured so far are named in the evidence, each with its own before-and-after, and they are comparable only with themselves. It is linear in rows × columns, so a 200-column Source of that height costs the better part of a minute on either mix.
  evidence: Reading every value is the frozen premise of the story (FR-9) and is not up for revision; the cost of doing so was. Two rounds of it are already paid — `score()` no longer allocates a row-index set per candidate, and a single pass now narrows the candidates to those a value could match, which took the NFR-3 shape from 1,499 ms to 991 ms and a 500,000-row integer column from 322 ms to 164 ms. The measured rate is about 2 million cells per second. What is left is real per-value work, and the routes out are all costly. **A worker is not one of them:** AD-15 forbids sending a dataset off-thread to compute on it, and R4 measured why — a structured clone of 100,000 rows blocks the sender for 109–132 ms and half a million for 511–627 ms, against 263–446 ms for the whole pipeline. That leaves making detection async so it can yield between columns, which turns `addSource` into an async command and touches the AD-10 command shape everywhere; or fusing the per-candidate walks into one pass over the values, which is maybe another third and makes `score` markedly harder to read. Both want a measurement harness in the repo rather than a script in a session, and neither belongs to a story about what a column is. **Re-measured 2026-08-02, story 4a review round 2: 2.08 s → 3.96 s at the same NFR-3 shape, roughly 1.9×**, and the ratio is the finding while the absolutes are hardware — it reproduces on a slower machine at 3.45 s → 4.74 s with a heavier column mix. Three structural causes, all of them the price of the five-kind competition: the four boolean pairs are scored on every column in the table; `affixScan` adds up to two extra passes per number reading wherever an affix character occurs anywhere in the column; and `marksPresent`'s early break — `present.size === MARKS.length` — is now effectively unreachable, because `MARKS` grew from **four marks to eight** (`. / - ,` plus `: % € $`) while an ordinary column contains four or five, so the loop that used to stop early walks to the end. **Every figure below is at the same NFR-3 shape of 100,000 rows × 20 columns, and the absolutes differ because the *column mix* differs. Two mixes have been used, and mixing their numbers is what made this entry unreadable, so they are named once and referred to by name.** **Mix A, "report-shaped"** — names, German amounts, four-digit dates, an ISO timestamp with a seven-digit fraction, clock times, `ja`/`nein`, percents, part numbers, integers, two-digit dates — costs the most per column, because almost every column in it gives some candidate something to chew on. **Mix B, "light"** — the story-3 shape, mostly plain numbers and names — costs about half as much. A figure from one mix says nothing about the other; a figure from one mix against another figure from the same mix is the finding.
    **Mix A, the three 2026-08-03 amendments (datetime clock, ISO basic format, the five two-digit date mirrors): 4.75–4.96 s before against 5.97–6.41 s after, ~1.3×**, in both orders of running, so the ordering is not the JIT. Two variants of the amended file isolate the cause: with the five new two-digit date patterns removed it is **4.75 s** — baseline — and with the always-scored ISO datetime candidate removed it is **6.24 s**, unchanged. So the whole regression is the five date mirrors and none of it is the ISO candidate that now scores on every column. `marksPresent` narrows date candidates by separator as designed, but the count on each separator grew and it grew by a different amount on each: **`.` from three to four, `/` from two to four, `-` from three to five** — and each is a real per-value walk. (This entry and the module header both said "four where it scored three" as if it held for all three separators; it held for the dot alone and understated the slash by half. Corrected 2026-08-03.)
    **Mix B, the fourth amendment (`dmy` declared as the preferred two-digit order): 2.28–2.50 s before against 2.26–2.54 s after — no measurable cost**, and that was measured rather than assumed: it adds no candidate and no walk, only a boolean on three existing entries and one comparison per short-year column. Four paired runs in alternating order, best of three per run: 2.28/2.30/2.28 against 2.29/2.26/2.29.
    **Mix A, the fifth amendment (`yy-MM-dd`, the mirror `yyyy-MM-dd` was owed): 2.65–2.70 s before against 2.72–2.79 s after — about +2.5 %**, four paired runs in alternating order, best of three per run: 2.65/2.69/2.68/2.66 against 2.72/2.74/2.74/2.74. The absolutes are lower than the 5.97–6.41 s above because the machine is not the one those were taken on; the pairing is what carries the finding. One candidate on one separator takes the dash from five patterns to **six** and adds nothing anywhere else, which is what a 2.5 % on a twenty-column table looks like — and it is the honest counterweight to the five mirrors' 1.3 %: the cost is per candidate per separator, so it is the *number of mirrors* that was expensive, not the idea of one.
    **Mix A, story 4b (one month-name candidate on a space separator): 2.40–2.45 s before against 2.52–2.54 s after — about +4.5 %**, four paired runs in alternating order, best of three per run: 2.40/2.40/2.45/2.42 against 2.52/2.52/2.54/2.54. The absolutes are again a different machine from the 5.97–6.41 s runs; the pairing carries the finding. **One candidate cost nearly twice what the `yy-MM-dd` one did (+2.5 %), and the reason is the separator rather than the candidate.** `MARKS` grew from eight marks to **nine** — `. / - ,` plus `: % € $` plus a space — and a space is the mark an ordinary report column is *least* likely to lack, so `marksPresent` skips this candidate almost nowhere, where the dash narrowing already skipped `yy-MM-dd` on most columns. That sharpens the whole entry's model: the cost is per candidate **per column it is not skipped on**, not per candidate.
